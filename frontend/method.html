Method

How it works

Single SPHEREx exposures cannot be compared directly: consecutive exposures of one spot are taken at different wavelengths. The tool averages the exposures of each visit so that visits can be compared.

Pipeline

1

Find the exposures

The IRSA TAP service lists every SPHEREx exposure that covers the position. For a new position this takes 20 to 30 seconds.

2

Read only the pixels needed

Level 2 images sit in a public Amazon S3 bucket. Byte-range requests fetch just the rows around the target: about 0.5 MB per exposure.

3

Group into visits

Exposures more than 30 days apart form separate epochs. Where coverage is continuous, it is cut into 60-day blocks.

4

Stack per band

For each of the six bands and each epoch, all exposures are averaged onto one north-up grid of 3.075″ pixels (6.15″, the detector’s own, for the 30′ and 1° fields).

Three ways to compare

Blink

Shows the epochs in turn. A source that moved jumps; a source that changed brightness flickers.

Difference

One epoch minus another. A moving source shows as a blue and red pair; everything that stayed the same cancels.

Stack

All epochs averaged together: the deepest image of the field, for finding faint sources.

Spectrum

Each exposure sees a spot through a different part of SPHEREx’s linear variable filter, so each exposure gives one point: a wavelength and a brightness. The points of all six detectors together cover 0.75 to 5 µm. They are coloured by epoch and can be downloaded as CSV. The chart can also show the median in each of the 102 spectral channels: 17 per band, equally wide in log wavelength.

The flux is an aperture sum minus the median of a surrounding ring. No aperture correction is applied. Exposures with saturated pixels in the aperture are skipped.

Catalogue marks

Gaia DR3 and CatWISE2020 sources are drawn where the catalogue’s proper motion puts each of them on the date of the epoch shown. A mark that follows a star from visit to visit confirms the motion independently. Sources with a well-measured motion above 0.15″ per year get a trail and a label.

Quality flags and zodiacal light

Every SPHEREx file carries a flag for each pixel (cosmic-ray hit, halo of a bright star, persistence, non-functional pixel and others) and a model of the zodiacal light. The Flags layer paints the pixels that are flagged in a fifth or more of the exposures of the visit shown; the panel on the left names the flags that occur and how much of the field they touch.

The stacks have the whole background of each exposure removed. “Zodi removed” takes out only the modelled zodiacal light, so what is left of the sky stays in the image, and the panel gives both levels in MJy/sr.

New data

SPHEREx data reach the archive every week. When the stored list of exposures for a position is more than seven days old, the tool shows what it has and asks the archive again in the background; new exposures are loaded as soon as they are found.

Known limits

The quality flags are shown, not applied: flagged pixels still enter the stacks. The stack is a clipped mean, which already drops a pixel that is off in a single exposure.

Very bright stars have empty (saturated) cores in some bands.

In the 30′ and 1° fields the other bands load only when chosen, the catalogue marks are the 400 brightest sources, and the corners can have fewer exposures than the centre.

The spectrum aperture is wider than the image resolution, so a neighbour within about 12″ is included in the measurement.

The motion of a marked source and the list of changes are measured in the browser from the stacked images. They are aids for the eye, not calibrated astrometry or photometry.
